Read properly, that transcript is **two checks for the price of one**, and the owner's four clicks are
what made it so:

  * **The veto holds** — steps 1, 2 and 3, three times over: the close was asked for while unsaved, the
    guard refused, and the program was handed a question it could put to the person. The window stayed
    up through all three refusals, which is the CL1 half of this row.
  * **Save comes back as Save, and the program chooses what to do with it** — `prompt|5|0`,
    `prompt|6|0`, `prompt|7|0`: button 0, the first of `"Save;Discard;Cancel"`. The probe has nothing to
    save to, so it kept the window up and asked once more; a program that *does* would write its file and
    then close. That is the *negative* direction of an unsaved-changes dialog, and it is the half a check
    written for the happy path usually misses.
  * **Corrected after the fact, and worth recording as such.** The first version of this file said those
    three clicks were **Cancel**. They were **Save**: index 0. The probe's own line — "that answer keeps
    the window up" — was the same sentence for Save and for Cancel, so the transcript could not tell them
    apart and the owner had to correct the record. The probe now names the button it was given, and it
    re-asks once rather than until told twice.
  * **Discard means close** — `prompt|8|1`: button 1, the program dropped its dirty mark, asked again,
    and step 5 shows the guard releasing the window. The process then ended **before its 150-second
    bound** rather than at it, which is what proves the window really went: a probe whose close had
    failed would have hit its own 120-second `FAIL` and said so.

So the five steps of this row happened in order, in one window, with one person: asked to close while
dirty, refused, questioned, answered, and closed.

Residual doubt.

  * **`run --no-jit`, `build` and `build --release` were not run** for this probe: each asks a person
    for a click, and the owner's limit for this session was one window. CL1's probe covers the guard on
    every tier and CL4's covers the sheet, so the composition is unverified on the compiled tiers
    rather than the parts.
  * **No fresh red.** Explained in `CL2.before.txt`: the row needed no code, so its check could not fail
    first, and it stands on the two recorded reds it composes. A reader should treat that as weaker
    evidence than a row with its own `.before.txt`, and this row's own failure text is a *citation*.
  * **The verdict's placement is a patch on a trap, and round 62 worked out which trap.** The two lines
    printed *after* `gpui::run` returned never appeared, while CL4's verdict — printed **inside** a frame
    before `quit()` — did. The reading that fits both transcripts is that **closing the last window ends
    the process inside `gpui::run`'s teardown rather than unwinding `main`**, so nothing flushes a
    block-buffered stdout; `quit()` returns normally and flushes, which is why CL4's line survived. Not
    proven by a discriminating experiment — a probe cannot easily tell "main never ran" from "main ran and
    the buffer was lost" — but it is the reading both transcripts agree on, and it gives a rule worth
    keeping: **a probe that closes its own window must print its verdict before it closes.** CL2's probe
    now does exactly that (step 5), and MN4's and MR5's use `quit()` rather than closing.
  * The probe re-asks **once** after Cancel or Save and then gives up with a `quit`, so a person who
    means Cancel twice sees the window stay and the run end. That is the probe's patience, not the
    binding's behaviour: `gpui::prompt` itself has no notion of a re-ask.
  * Nothing here exercises the *platform's own* close request — the traffic lights, ⌘W — which cannot be
    synthesized; CL1's probe has the same hole and says so.
