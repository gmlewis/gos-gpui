What the source said: `gpui::Window::on_window_should_close` existed at HEAD
(`gpui-0.2.2/src/window.rs:4329`) and was unexposed. It is a plain method —
`&self`, `&App`, and a `Fn(&mut Window, &mut App) -> bool + 'static` — so the only
obstacle was that a binding function has no `Window` to register it on. gpui wires
it straight to the platform window's close request
(`self.platform_window.on_should_close(...)`, `platform.rs:496`), which is what
makes it a real interception rather than a binding-side convention.

**A platform close request cannot be synthesized**, and the probe could not check
anything if it could not produce one. So the binding routes its *own* close paths
through the same handler — `close_window` and `close_window_in` ask before removing
a window — and says so on `gpui::on_window_should_close`, where a reader will find
it. Upstream's `remove_window` does not do that; this is a deliberate departure, and
it is the only reason the row could be checked at all.

The probe is two-directional for the same reason a one-directional one is not a
check: a guard that always refuses is indistinguishable from a close that never
worked. Two children, one refusing and one agreeing, closed by name from a third
window that then watches whether each kept reporting. Both breaks were run:

    BREAK A — every close allowed
    exit=0
    FAIL CL1 the refusing guard was ignored: its window stopped reporting at 6

    BREAK B — no close allowed
    exit=0
    FAIL CL1 the agreeing guard was ignored: its window reported again, 8 then 12

Each break fails exactly one direction and passes the other, which is what says
the two assertions are measuring different things. Restored immediately; the
